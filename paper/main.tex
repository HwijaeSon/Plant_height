\author[inst1]{Yeoul Lim}

\begin{abstract}
Accurate prediction of plant growth requires models that integrate genotypic variation and environmental responses from sparsely sampled phenotypes. This study presents \model{}, a genotype- and environment-conditioned latent neural ordinary differential equation model for continuous-time growth prediction. The model combines a learned latent dynamical system with logistic derivative and carrying-capacity regularization in phenotype space. Growth trajectories are predicted from genotype, time, and environmental covariates without phenotype measurements from the target trajectory as inputs. Evaluation on wheat, unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV)-derived maize height, and Arabidopsis stem length yielded mean test root mean square errors (RMSEs) of $0.03032$~m, $56.68$ relative UAV-height units, and $2.800$~cm, respectively, corresponding to relative RMSEs of $10.24\%$, $18.40\%$, and $14.06\%$. PhytoODE achieved lower mean test errors than the evaluated baselines and matched unregularized latent ODE controls. These datasets encompassed 19 wheat genotypes, 402 maize genotypes, and Arabidopsis trajectories with four observations per plant. A further experiment comprised 943 hypocotyl-length measurements collected from five Arabidopsis genotypes at seven time points under a 12-h light/12-h dark photoperiod at 23~$^{\circ}$C. With binary illumination as an input, PhytoODE achieved a test RMSE of $0.346$~mm and a relative RMSE of $6.85\%$ for held-out replicate means, the lowest among the six evaluated models. The results demonstrate the applicability of latent continuous-time modelling across field seasons, genotype-panel sizes, and phenotyping resolutions under the specified evaluation protocols. The hypocotyl dataset and analysis code will be publicly released with the article.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Temporal phenotyping enables plant growth to be characterized as a dynamic process rather than solely by endpoint traits. Imaging and sensor systems provide repeated measurements of crop development \citep{zhang2020phenotyping,kim2023monitoring,ehret2011tomato}, while time-series remote sensing and image-generation methods support crop mapping and growth forecasting \citep{wang2021sentinel,drees2021brassica}. Integrating these observations into predictive models requires representation of the interactions among genotype, developmental stage, and environmental conditions. However, longitudinal phenotyping remains resource-intensive, and sampling frequency varies substantially across experimental systems, from daily field measurements to a small number of manual or destructive observations.

Machine learning has been widely applied to crop prediction using environmental and phenotypic time series. Applications include wheat and maize yield forecasting from vegetation indices \citep{nagy2018forecasting}, farm-level and durum-wheat prediction \citep{tesfaye2021wheat,chergui2022durum}, maize hybrid performance prediction \citep{sarijaloo2021corn}, and recurrent modelling of UAV and meteorological observations \citep{wang2025sugarbeet}. Related approaches estimate crop responses from temperature or limited climatic information \citep{sharma2022evapotranspiration,singh2023eto}, grapevine phenology from climate records \citep{fasihi2025phenology}, and crop stress from biological signals \citep{wen2025stress}. Deep networks have also been used to identify weather-sensitive developmental windows in spring wheat \citep{benke2025weather}. Nevertheless, the data requirements and limited biological constraints of purely empirical models can restrict their reliability when phenotyping is sparse or environmental conditions vary.

Genotype--environment interactions introduce additional heterogeneity into growth trajectories. Multi-environment analyses involve incomplete genotype-by-environment observations \citep{arciniegas2020imputation}, nonlinear responses \citep{patil2023genotype}, and environment-dependent genetic effects \citep{li2026plasticity}. Environmental regulation also influences water use, morphology, and biomass accumulation across scales \citep{gao2024ipecm,salagovic2025tomato,zhang2026lettuce}. A general growth-prediction framework therefore requires parameter sharing across genotypes while retaining genotype-specific responses to environmental covariates.

Hybrid mechanistic and machine-learning approaches incorporate biological structure into predictive models. Process-based simulations have been integrated with hyperspectral observations \citep{wang2025gecros}, remote sensing and machine learning for wheat prediction \citep{kheir2025hybrid}, and deep learning for wheat biomass dynamics \citep{chen2026biomass}. Such integration is particularly relevant to limited training data \citep{dhal2022aquaponic}, parsimonious model design \citep{saravi2021variable}, and interpretable agricultural prediction \citep{ryo2022xai,ennaji2023nutrient}. Reservoir and pretrained sequence models offer complementary approaches to vegetation-index forecasting \citep{olamofe2025ndvi}. For plant-height prediction, \citet{shao2026pinn} introduced Logi-PINN, which combines an LSTM with logistic growth regularization. This approach demonstrates the potential of biological priors, although a scalar logistic reference may have different explanatory capacity across genotypes, environments, organs, and species.

Neural ordinary differential equations parameterize continuous-time dynamics using learned vector fields \citep{chen2018neuralode}. Latent ODEs extend this formulation to unobserved states and accommodate irregularly sampled observations \citep{rubanova2019latentode}. State augmentation can improve expressiveness and numerical behaviour \citep{dupont2019augmented}, while neural controlled differential equations represent continuous-time responses to covariates \citep{kidger2020neuralcde}. These formulations complement physics-informed learning \citep{raissi2019pinn,karniadakis2021piml} and universal differential equations that combine mechanistic and learned components \citep{rackauckas2020ude}. Their application to genotype-conditioned plant growth nevertheless requires evaluation across agricultural datasets with different environmental conditions, genetic diversity, and temporal sampling densities.

This study develops \model{}, a latent neural ODE framework with shared genotype-conditioned dynamics and logistic regularization of the predicted phenotype. The methodological contribution is a continuous-time model that predicts genotype-specific growth trajectories from genotype, time, and a known environmental sequence, without using target-trajectory phenotype measurements as inputs. Its empirical evaluation comprises a wheat benchmark aligned with \citet{shao2026pinn}, a maize benchmark spanning 402 genotypes, an external Arabidopsis stem-length dataset with four observations per plant, and a hypocotyl dataset collected in this study. The wheat and maize experiments evaluate prediction across growing seasons, the Arabidopsis experiment assesses performance under sparse temporal sampling, and the hypocotyl experiment extends environmental conditioning from temperature to illumination. The latter dataset, which contains unequal numbers of replicate measurements, is intended for public release. These complementary evaluations examine variation in species, organ, measurement scale, genotype-panel size, and sampling design.


\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Prediction task}

Let $g\in\{1,\ldots,G\}$ denote genotype, $\mathbf u_g(t)$ a known environmental scenario, and $y_g(t_i)$ the observed growth phenotype at a subset of times $\mathcal{O}_g$. The environmental input is temperature in the first three datasets and binary illumination state in the author-collected hypocotyl experiment. The model predicts a complete trajectory $\hat y_g(t)$ on a common integration grid. Missing phenotype times do not contribute to the data loss. The principal task is trajectory-level generalization: to a held-out year for wheat and maize and to new biological replicate plants for Arabidopsis stem length. The hypocotyl experiment evaluates means of held-out replicate groups at the same observed times. In all four datasets, the evaluated genotype labels are present during training.

The prediction task assumes that the environmental sequence over the study interval is known. The initial-state encoder conditions on this sequence without access to phenotype measurements from the evaluated trajectory. Accordingly, the formulation describes scenario-conditioned trajectory prediction; real-time forecasting would require restricting the environmental inputs to information available at the forecast origin.

\subsection{\model{} architecture: genotype- and environment-conditioned latent dynamics}

Each genotype is represented by a $d_e$-dimensional embedding $\mathbf e_g$ learned from a one-hot vector. An LSTM encoder reads the environmental channels and normalized time in reverse temporal order and produces the initial latent state,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf z_g(0)=E_{\phi}\!\left(\{\mathbf u_g(\tau_j),\tau_j\}_{j=1}^{J},\mathbf e_g\right),
 \qquad \mathbf z_g(0)\in\mathbb R^{d_z}.
 \label{eq:encoder}
\end{equation}
The latent state evolves according to
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\mathbf z_g}{d\tau}
 =3\tanh\!\left[f_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf z_g(\tau),\mathbf u_g(\tau),\mathbf e_g,\tau\right)\right],
 \qquad \tau\in[0,1],
 \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\theta}$ is a hyperbolic-tangent multilayer perceptron (MLP). For temperature-conditioned experiments, standardized temperature at intermediate solver stages is obtained by linear interpolation, and a fixed-step fourth-order Runge--Kutta solver uses one substep per daily interval. The hypocotyl experiment uses a 3-h grid with piecewise-constant illumination, changing state at the 12-h light boundaries, as detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:lightmodel}. Finally, an MLP decoder maps the latent trajectory to the phenotype,
\begin{equation}
 \hat y_g(\tau)=\operatorname{LeakyReLU}_{0.01}\!\left[D_{\psi}(\mathbf z_g(\tau))\right].
 \label{eq:decoder}
\end{equation}
For wheat, model selection based on validation RMSE yielded a 16-dimensional latent state, four-dimensional genotype embedding, one ODE hidden layer of width 16, decoder width 16, and LSTM encoder width 8, giving 1,655 trainable parameters. The maize architecture comprised latent dimension 8, genotype-embedding dimension 8, one ODE hidden layer of width 16, decoder width 32, and encoder width 32, giving 9,003 parameters. Arabidopsis used the initial architecture: latent dimension 16, genotype-embedding dimension 4, two ODE hidden layers of width 32, decoder width 32, and encoder width 16, giving 4,607 parameters. All experiments used the same model formulation, with dataset-specific capacity selection where a validation search was performed.

\subsection{Observable-space logistic regularization and derivative computation}

Logistic regularization constrains the predicted phenotype while preserving the flexibility of the latent vector field. Let
\begin{equation}
 G_{\psi}(\mathbf z)
 =\operatorname{LeakyReLU}_{0.01}\!\left[D_{\psi}(\mathbf z)\right]
\end{equation}
denote the complete scalar decoder in Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoder}, including its output activation, and let $\mathbf F_{\theta}$ denote the right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ode}. The decoder has no explicit time input, so its dependence on time is entirely through the latent trajectory. For trajectory $n$, belonging to genotype $g(n)$, we therefore have
\begin{equation}
 \hat y_n(\tau)=G_{\psi}\!\left(\mathbf z_n(\tau)\right),
 \qquad
 \frac{d\hat y_n}{d\tau}
 =\nabla_{\mathbf z}G_{\psi}\!\left(\mathbf z_n(\tau)\right)^{\!\top}
 \mathbf F_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf z_n(\tau),\mathbf u_n(\tau),
 \mathbf e_{g(n)},\tau\right).
 \label{eq:decoder_chain_tau}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:decoder_chain_tau} gives the directional derivative of the decoder along the latent vector field. The logistic constraint is therefore applied in phenotype space, without assigning a biological growth variable to an individual latent coordinate.

The ODE is solved on normalized time $\tau\in[0,1]$, whereas the reference growth rate uses physical time (days for the first three datasets and hours for hypocotyls). If $t=t_0+S\tau$, where $S=t_{\mathrm{last}}-t_0$ is the physical duration of the integration interval, a second application of the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\hat y_n}{dt}
 =\frac{1}{S}\frac{d\hat y_n}{d\tau}
 =\frac{1}{S}
 \nabla_{\mathbf z}G_{\psi}\!\left(\mathbf z_n(\tau)\right)^{\!\top}
 \mathbf F_{\theta}\!\left(\mathbf z_n(\tau),\mathbf u_n(\tau),
 \mathbf e_{g(n)},\tau\right).
 \label{eq:decoder_chain_day}
\end{equation}
The integration durations $S$ were 169, 95, and 22 days for wheat, maize, and Arabidopsis stem length, respectively, and 72 hours for hypocotyl length. Division by $S$ expresses the predicted growth rate in physical time units, ensuring consistency with the logistic reference.

The derivative in Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoder_chain_day} is evaluated by automatic differentiation. At each collocation node $(n,j)$, the vector field is evaluated at the integrated latent state, $\mathbf F_{\theta}(\mathbf z_{nj},\mathbf u_{nj},\mathbf e_{g(n)},\tau_j)$, and reverse-mode differentiation provides the decoder gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf z}G_{\psi}(\mathbf z_{nj})$. This gradient is obtained from the scalar sum
\begin{equation}
 Q=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}G_{\psi}(\mathbf z_{nj}),
 \qquad
 \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mathbf z_{nj}}
 =\nabla_{\mathbf z}G_{\psi}(\mathbf z_{nj}),
 \label{eq:autodiff_decoder}
\end{equation}
where the equality follows from the independent application of the decoder to each trajectory--time pair. The inner product of the decoder gradient and vector field yields $d\hat y_n/d\tau$; division by $S$ then gives $d\hat y_n/dt$. This computation includes the piecewise-differentiable LeakyReLU output activation and requires neither observed growth-rate targets nor numerical differentiation of sparse phenotype measurements.

The derivative evaluation and fixed-step Runge--Kutta integration remain in the computational graph. Differentiation through the residual therefore propagates gradients to the decoder, latent vector field, initial-state encoder, and genotype embedding, as well as to the logistic parameter network defined below. The derivative-based objective can consequently be optimized from length observations alone.

For the temperature-conditioned datasets, a small MLP maps each genotype embedding to two constrained logistic parameters,
\begin{equation}
 r_g=\sigma(a_g), \qquad K_g=1+\tanh(b_g),
 \label{eq:logistic_parameters}
\end{equation}
where $r_g\in(0,1)$ day$^{-1}$ and $K_g\in(0,2)$ in the units used to train the phenotype output. Those units are metres for wheat and Arabidopsis and training-scaled relative-height units for maize; maize predictions are multiplied by the training-set scale factor for reporting. The pointwise logistic residual is
\begin{equation}
 \varepsilon_{nj}
 =\frac{d\hat y_n(t_j)}{dt}
 -r_{g(n)}\hat y_n(t_j)
 \left(1-\frac{\hat y_n(t_j)}{K_{g(n)}}\right),
\end{equation}
and the corresponding physics loss is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}
 =\frac{1}{N J}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\varepsilon_{nj}^{2}.
 \label{eq:physics}
\end{equation}
In the temperature-conditioned experiments, the $J$ daily integration nodes served as collocation points for each training trajectory. Equation~\eqref{eq:physics} was evaluated at these nodes, including dates without phenotype observations, but not at intermediate Runge--Kutta stages. Validation and test targets were excluded from the training objective. For Arabidopsis, the data term used four observed stem lengths per plant, whereas the physics term regularized the daily predictions across the full 23-day grid. Collocation at unobserved dates introduces a prior on trajectory shape without imputing phenotype targets.

Equation~\eqref{eq:physics} defines a soft constraint on the decoded growth rate. Trajectory generation remains governed by the learned latent vector field, allowing departures from logistic dynamics when supported by the observed data.

For the three temperature-conditioned datasets, the observed-data term is the mean of the per-trajectory masked RMSE,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}
 =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
 \sqrt{\frac{\sum_j m_{nj}(\hat y_{nj}-y_{nj})^2}
 {\sum_j m_{nj}}},
 \label{eq:data_loss}
\end{equation}
and the full objective is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{data}}
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{phys}}
 +\lambda_K\mathcal L_K,
 \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_K
 =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}
 \left|\max_j\hat y_{nj}-K_{g(n)}\right|
\end{equation}
penalizes disagreement between the genotype-specific carrying capacity and the maximum predicted phenotype within each training trajectory. The data term uses the observation mask $m_{nj}$, whereas both regularization terms use the complete prediction grid. We write $\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}}\equiv\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}$ for the derivative-residual coefficient. The reported $(\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}},\lambda_K)$ values are $(3.16227766,0.1)$ for wheat, $(0.5,0.5)$ for maize, and $(0.5,0.1)$ for Arabidopsis; their selection is described in Section~\ref{subsec:physicsablationmethods}. Both regularizers were applied from the first training epoch. Monotonicity and explicit $r_g$ penalties were assigned zero weight, and Adam weight decay was set to $10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Wheat field dataset and year-held-out protocol}
\label{subsec:wheatdata}

The wheat data were obtained from the Field Phenotyping Platform experiments described by \citet{roth2024thermal} and processed following \citet{shao2026pinn}. Nineteen genotypes present in all four seasons (2018, 2019, 2021, and 2022) were retained. Height trajectories were aligned to a 285-day grid, and the first 115 positions were removed, leaving 170 daily positions. Missing positions before the first measurement were filled with the minimum observed height for that trajectory, as in the reference implementation. Air temperature at 2~m was the environmental input and was standardized using the training years.

The reference scoring mask was retained for comparability with the published benchmark. Of the 1,368 wheat test positions, 475 were imputed positions preceding the first observation. These positions contributed to both RMSE and the denominator of relative RMSE. Wheat errors therefore quantify performance under the reference preprocessing protocol and are not directly equivalent to the observation-only metrics used for the other datasets.

The model was trained on 88 plot-level trajectories from 2018 and 2019. Replicate plots were averaged within genotype--year combinations for evaluation, yielding 38 training curves and 19 curves in each held-out year. Following split~0 of the reference implementation, 2022 was used for validation and 2021 for testing. This partition matched the training, validation, and test years of the reference models.

The model was optimized for 1,500 epochs with Adam, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and gradient clipping at 1.0. Validation RMSE was evaluated every ten epochs; checkpoint selection began after epoch 600 and used the mean of the ten most recent validation evaluations.

\subsubsection{Wheat capacity and hyperparameter selection}

Model capacity was selected using 2022 validation RMSE. Ten configurations varied latent dimension (4--16), genotype-embedding dimension (2--4), vector-field width (8--32) and depth (one or two hidden layers), decoder width (8--32), and encoder width (4--16). Screening used seeds 1 and 2. Pareto-efficient candidates were evaluated with learning rates $2\times10^{-3}$, $5\times10^{-3}$, and $10^{-2}$ and derivative-residual weights 0, 0.5, 2, and 5. The selected architecture contained 1,655 parameters and initially used learning rate $10^{-2}$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}}=2$, and $\lambda_K=0.1$. With this architecture and training schedule fixed, joint coefficient selection based on three-seed mean validation RMSE yielded $(3.16227766,0.1)$ (Section~\ref{subsec:physicsablationmethods}). This configuration is reported in Table~\ref{tab:wheat}. A 393-parameter model was included as an additional parameter-efficiency comparison.

\subsection{Maize UAV dataset and year-held-out protocol}
\label{subsec:maizedata}

The maize dataset was obtained from the UAV phenotyping study of \citet{sweet2024maize}, which surveyed approximately 500 inbred lines over four field seasons. Quality control retained 402 genotypes observed in every year with unambiguous plot identifiers, comprising 3,072 plot-level trajectories and 31,894 height observations. The sampling unit was a repeatedly measured field plot. The response variable was relative UAV-derived height; the released data do not support conversion to absolute length units.

Trajectories were represented on a 0--95 day-after-planting grid, with the 5--17 observed dates per plot contributing to the loss. Daily mean temperature, approximated from station minimum and maximum temperatures, was standardized using the training years. The model was trained on 1,533 plot trajectories from 2018--2019. For evaluation, available replicate plots were averaged within genotype, year, and date, yielding 804 genotype--year curves for training evaluation, 402 validation curves for 2020, and 402 test curves for 2021. This partition evaluated prediction in an unobserved growing season across 402 genotypes represented during training.

Hyperparameter optimization used 2020 validation RMSE and comprised 95 training runs across 67 configurations, including comparisons of 1,500-, 3,000-, and 4,500-epoch training budgets. Fourteen configurations were evaluated with all three seeds. The selected model used 3,000 epochs, latent dimension 8, genotype-embedding dimension 8, vector-field width 16, decoder width 32, encoder width 32, learning rate $10^{-2}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, and $(\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}},\lambda_K)=(0.5,0.5)$. This initial configuration and its three checkpoints were fixed before evaluation on the 2021 test set. The reported model retains these checkpoints; its relationship to the subsequent coefficient search is specified in Section~\ref{subsec:physicsablationmethods}.

\subsection{External Arabidopsis dataset with sparse temporal sampling}
\label{subsec:arabdata}

The external dataset was extracted from the supplementary workbook of \citet{naghani2024arabidopsis}. Primary inflorescence stem length was measured for nine genotypes (Col-0, \textit{phyB}, \textit{pif3}, \textit{pif4}, \textit{pif7-1}, \textit{pif7-2}, \textit{pif3 pif7}, \textit{pifq}, and 35S::PIF4), two temperature regimes, and ten plants per genotype--temperature combination. Plants were grown under long days (16-h light/8-h dark). The normal ambient-temperature regime (nAT) was 21/18~$^{\circ}$C day/night, and the high ambient-temperature regime (hAT) was 28/24~$^{\circ}$C day/night. Mean day/night temperature was used as the environmental input.

Each of the 180 plants was measured at four time points: 29, 36, 39, and 49 days after sowing (DAS) under nAT and 27, 30, 37, and 41 DAS under hAT, yielding 720 measurements. All trajectories were represented on a common 27--49 DAS integration grid; only observed dates contributed to the data loss and evaluation metrics. Plant identifiers 1--6 were assigned to training, 7--8 to validation, and 9--10 to testing. Neural models were trained on 108 individual-plant trajectories. Validation and test metrics were each computed on 18 replicate-averaged genotype--temperature trajectories with four observations. This partition evaluated generalization to held-out biological replicates within the genotype and temperature combinations represented during training.

For Arabidopsis, PhytoODE was trained with the architecture specified above and the same objective as wheat, using $S=22$ days. Training comprised 1,500 epochs with seeds 1--3. Checkpoints were selected from epoch 300 onward using a ten-evaluation moving average of validation RMSE. The four observations per plant provided an evaluation under sparse temporal sampling, without imposing additional missingness patterns.

\subsection{Physics-coefficient study and matched loss ablation}
\label{subsec:physicsablationmethods}

For the three temperature-conditioned datasets, \model{} was compared with an identical latent neural ODE trained with $\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}}=\lambda_K=0$, denoted Latent ODE (no physics). Architecture, initial weights, training budget, optimizer, and validation-based checkpoint selection were held constant. The auxiliary logistic parameter network was retained to preserve identical initialization, although it received no gradient when both penalties were disabled. Optimizer weight decay remained $10^{-4}$. A separate capacity-only ablation retained $\lambda_K>0$ with $\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}}=0$.

Joint selection of the two regularization coefficients followed an initial search over $\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}}$, with all other hyperparameters held constant. For each dataset, a $6\times5$ grid and four geometric refinements were evaluated with seed 1. The three best-performing pairs with both coefficients positive, together with the best pair for each single-penalty model, were evaluated with seeds 2 and 3. Selection was based on three-seed mean validation RMSE. The joint analysis incorporated completed trials and 112 additional full-length training runs, yielding $(3.16227766,0.1)$ for wheat, $(500,0.5)$ for maize, and $(0.5,0.1)$ for Arabidopsis. These pairs were unchanged from the initial coefficient search.

For maize, the original $(0.5,0.5)$ configuration was retained after examination of the coefficient-search results. Its validation RMSE was 47.247, compared with 44.943 for $(500,0.5)$, and the corresponding test RMSEs were 56.684 and 60.526. The reported configuration was therefore not the validation optimum of the subsequent search. Because test results had been inspected before these coefficient analyses, the comparisons do not provide independent confirmation on previously unexamined test data. The unregularized control was not independently retuned.

\subsection{Baselines and evaluation}

Each temperature-conditioned dataset was evaluated against the unregularized latent ODE control and five baseline methods: genotype-specific logistic ODE (Logi-ODE), temperature-informed ODE (Temp-ODE), random forest (RF), one-hot LSTM-NN, and Logi-PINN. Wheat LSTM-NN and Logi-PINN results were obtained from the first three seeds of the reference outputs of \citet{shao2026pinn}; the two process models and RF were fitted using the same partition and scoring mask. Maize and Arabidopsis baselines followed the corresponding partition, input, and normalization protocols. Logistic parameters were estimated from training data alone. The additional maize hyperparameter search was restricted to PhytoODE; the comparison therefore reflects model-specific training procedures with unequal optimization budgets.

The primary metric is the mean of the RMSEs computed separately for each replicate-averaged trajectory over the evaluation mask, following \citet{shao2026pinn}. Scale-normalized performance is additionally reported as relative RMSE,
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{rRMSE}_s(\%)=100\,\frac{\mathrm{RMSE}_s}{\bar y_s},
 \label{eq:rrmse}
\end{equation}
where $\bar y_s$ is the pooled mean of all masked targets in partition $s$. The denominator is identical across models within each dataset and partition and is used exclusively for reporting; normalization does not affect model rankings or checkpoint selection. Tables report RMSE / rRMSE (\%). Neural models and RF were evaluated with seeds 1--3, and results are expressed as mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation. Logi-ODE and Temp-ODE were fitted once by deterministic least squares. Figures for the temperature-conditioned experiments display PhytoODE, the five baseline methods, and observed targets using a consistent model order and colour scheme. The unregularized latent ODE is included in the tables. Predicted curves are displayed within the observation interval of each sample, with intervening segments representing model predictions.

\subsection{Wheat: year-held-out plant-height prediction}
\label{subsec:wheatresults}

The 1,655-parameter model yielded a test RMSE / rRMSE of $0.03032\pm0.00066$~m / $10.24\pm0.22\%$, corresponding to RMSE reductions of 46.5\% relative to LSTM-NN, 53.8\% relative to Logi-PINN, and 1.91\% relative to the matched unregularized latent ODE. The unregularized model attained a lower training error, whereas \model{} achieved lower validation and test errors, consistent with a modest benefit from logistic regularization under this evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Maize: year-held-out prediction across 402 genotypes}
\label{subsec:maizeresults}

With $(\lambda_{\mathrm{ODE}},\lambda_K)=(0.5,0.5)$, \model{} achieved the lowest mean test error among the evaluated baselines and the unregularized latent ODE control (Table~\ref{tab:maize}). Test RMSE / rRMSE was $56.68\pm4.37$ relative UAV-height units / $18.40\pm1.42\%$. Hyperparameter optimization reduced test rRMSE by 28.8\% from the initial $25.84\pm4.26\%$. The final error was 42.6\% lower than that of LSTM-NN ($32.06\pm3.31\%$), the strongest of the five baseline methods on the test set. RF attained the lowest training error and the highest test error. LSTM-NN ranked first on the 2020 validation set, whereas PhytoODE ranked first on the 2021 test set. This change in ranking highlights the importance of evaluating performance across multiple growing seasons.

\subsection{Arabidopsis: external validation with four observations per plant}
\label{subsec:arabresults}

\model{} achieved the lowest mean validation and test errors on the Arabidopsis stem-length dataset (Table~\ref{tab:arabidopsis}). \subsection{Contribution of the physics regularizers}
\label{subsec:physicsablationresults}

The reported PhytoODE configurations reduced mean test RMSE relative to the matched unregularized latent ODE by 1.91\% for wheat, 16.37\% for maize, and 6.46\% for Arabidopsis (Tables~\ref{tab:wheat}--\ref{tab:arabidopsis}). These comparisons evaluate the combined derivative-residual and carrying-capacity penalties. The capacity-only maize model achieved a slightly lower test RMSE / rRMSE ($55.83\pm6.15$ / $18.12\pm2.00\%$) than the model with both penalties, despite a higher validation RMSE (48.143 versus 47.247). Thus, the improvements over the unregularized control cannot be attributed exclusively to the derivative residual, and adding this term did not consistently improve test performance relative to capacity regularization alone. Interpretation is also limited by the absence of independent hyperparameter optimization for the unregularized controls and by prior inspection of the test sets.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presented \model{}, a genotype- and environment-conditioned latent neural ODE that combines learned continuous-time dynamics with logistic regularization in phenotype space. PhytoODE achieved the lowest mean test RMSE among the evaluated baseline methods and matched unregularized controls on wheat, maize, and Arabidopsis stem length, with rRMSEs of $10.24\pm0.22\%$, $18.40\pm1.42\%$, and $14.06\pm0.08\%$, respectively. The wheat experiment demonstrated prediction across growing seasons with a 1,655-parameter model. The maize evaluation extended the formulation to 402 genotypes and 3,072 field-plot trajectories, while the Arabidopsis experiment established its applicability to four observations per plant. These evaluations indicate that shared latent dynamics can represent genotype-dependent growth across distinct phenotyping scales and sampling densities.

The hypocotyl experiment extended environmental conditioning to binary illumination at constant temperature. Using 943 measurements from five genotypes at seven time points, PhytoODE achieved a test RMSE of $0.346$~mm and an rRMSE of $6.85\%$, compared with $7.14\%$ for Logistic-PINN and $7.28\%$ for the unregularized latent ODE. This was the lowest mean test error among the six selected models. The result concerns prediction of held-out replicate means under a shared photoperiod, with additional hyperparameter optimization and the illumination input applied to PhytoODE. The dataset and analysis workflow will be released with the article to support reproducible evaluation of growth prediction from sparse, unequally replicated measurements.

Several aspects of the evaluation limit the scope of these conclusions. Wheat and maize hyperparameters were selected using a single validation year, and the coefficient analysis reused previously inspected test sets. The reported maize configuration also differed from the subsequent validation optimum. All evaluated genotype identities and controlled temperature regimes were represented during training, and the Arabidopsis test trajectories each averaged two plants. Further evaluation should use independent cohorts, multiple year partitions, independently optimized controls, and held-out genotypes or environmental regimes.

The experiments assessed predictions within the observed time interval and assumed access to the environmental sequence over that interval. Real-time forecasting would require an encoder restricted to environmental information available at the forecast origin, together with evaluation at predefined forecast horizons. These extensions would clarify the potential of PhytoODE for prospective agricultural phenotyping and growth monitoring.
